Architecture

Where the operator's job ends and the platform's begins.

Three drawings that carry most of the argument: what kagent owns against what you still own, what one request actually does on the wire, and what you give up by bringing your own agent.

The boundary

Seven layers, each split down the middle.

kagent does not own a clean bottom half of the stack. Every layer is divided, and the right-hand side is ordinary platform work — quota, egress, secrets, RBAC, backup, identity.

Figure 1. Teal is kagent's half; amber is yours. The two rows worth singling out are the ones people assume are handled: the tool server's RBAC is the agent's real authority — approval gates and curated tool lists constrain what an agent decides, while the credential doing the work belongs to a separate workload — and egress to the model provider is traffic your platform has to permit, because an agent with no route to its model doesn't start.

One request

A tool call is a network call. Delegation is the same shape.

The model↔agent cycle isn't kagent-specific; every framework has it. What matters operationally is that each tool call leaves the pod — to an MCP server or another agent, each holding its own credential — and that the number of round trips is decided by the model at request time, not by anything in your manifest.

Figure 2. Every agent has its own Service, so agent-to-agent calls go pod to pod and the controller is an optional front door, not a hop — keeping request traffic off the controller keeps the data path decoupled from reconciliation. The loop ends not on a signal but on the absence of another function call. That's also where cost and latency come from, and why neither is readable off a YAML file.

The fork

Declarative or BYO — and what BYO actually costs.

spec.type is an enum with exactly two values. The real choice isn't "who writes the loop" — it's how much of the platform you give up to own it.

Figure 3. spec.byo has exactly one field — a Deployment spec. Meanwhile modelConfig, tools, memory, context and the approval gate all live under spec.declarative. That follows once you see it: the operator can't compact a context it didn't build, or gate a tool call it never dispatched. ARIA runs 11 declarative agents and 2 BYO — LangGraph and Strands — so the container contract has been proven twice, and the heuristic holds: go declarative unless you can name what's unusual about your loop.

What's actually deployed

Fourteen namespaces, forty-eight deployments.

The agents are the visible part. Most of what makes them operable is everything around them — and all of it arrives through the same two deploy layers: Terraform for AWS, ArgoCD for everything on the cluster. There is no third "install it by hand just this once" layer.

Dev and demo surfaces

The test that decides where anything goes: if the cluster were deleted and terraform apply re-ran with ArgoCD syncing from git, would this come back exactly, with no manual step? If not, it's in the wrong place. Two principled narrowings live in Terraform rather than ArgoCD — the irreducible bootstrap kernel, since ArgoCD can't deploy ArgoCD, and secret material generation, since a public repo can't hold values.
